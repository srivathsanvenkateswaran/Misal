Misal · app mark · five directions

Pick a logo

Five marks, each drawn from something actually distinctive about a misal rather than from “bowl of food”. Reply with a letter. Mixing is fine — “D but with B’s layers” is a useful answer.

Judge them small. An app mark spends its life at 32 px in a dock and 16 px in a tab, so the size ladder under each option matters more than the big version. The single-colour row is the real test: a mark that only works in five colours is a weak mark.

C vs E

The dock test

The question isn’t which mark is nicer in isolation — both are. It’s which one you can find in a quarter of a second. An icon never has to explain the product, because nobody meets it before installing; its whole job is retrieval. So the honest test is each mark among the apps it will actually sit beside, which are all finance apps.

E · outlined, third from left — and you had to look for the outline to find it.

C · same position. You found it before you read this line.

There is also an argument against E that has nothing to do with taste. A ring is always full. A donut icon quietly promises that everything is accounted for — that the whole circle is known. Misal’s entire stance is the opposite: it withholds figures it cannot support, shows hatching where a holding is snapshot-only, and says “not priced” rather than ₹0. The one mark that states completeness on every screen would be the icon.

The counter-argument for E is real: it tells a stranger on GitHub that this is a portfolio tool. But Misal isn’t discovered in an app store — it’s handed to people who already know what it is. Category-signalling buys little; memorability buys a lot. And the name is already the hook. “Misal” is a food word, and a bowl of sev finishes that sentence where an abstract ring argues with it.

Nothing is wasted either way: E’s ring is a better in-app motif than an app icon. Keep it for the allocation view, where a ring drawn from real data — and visibly incomplete when the data is — says something true. Different jobs, both filled.

A

Tarri

The oil slick. That fiery layer floating on the surface is the one thing nothing else in Indian food looks like — you can identify a misal from it alone, from across a room. Read as data: the things sitting on top of everything else, and the small separate beads that haven’t merged in.

Risk: the most abstract of the five. Someone who doesn’t know the dish sees a dark disc with an orange shape — pleasant, but not obviously anything.

Katori

The layers, but held in a bowl so they read as a serving rather than as a colour chart. This is the metaphor the project already commits to in its own README — components prepared separately, assembled into one bowl, each keeping its character — and the bands are also, exactly, an allocation. The closest thing here to a literal picture of the product.

Risk: closest to the icon you already have, so it may feel like a refinement rather than a redesign. The rim is doing a lot of work — without it, it’s the current icon again.

C

Sev

The heap of fine crisp strands piled over the rim — the texture that makes a misal photograph look like a misal. Overflowing the bowl is the point: more than the container holds. The one mark here with any energy in it; the others are all static.

Risk: texture is the first thing to die when you shrink something. Check the 16 px rung honestly — if the strands turn to mush there, this is a wordmark companion, not an app icon.

D

Misal pav

The bowl and the pav — because it is never just the bowl. Two objects of different size and weight is the most logo-like arrangement of the five: it has a subject and a counterweight, which is what makes a mark stick. The pav also happens to be the only circle, so it reads as the round total beside the stacked parts.

Risk: two objects halve the size of each. At 16 px the pav becomes a dot and the layers collapse into a smudge.

E

Rim

The bowl from directly above: a steel katori rim, the toppings as unequal arcs, the dark depth of the usal in the middle. This is the one place where the dish and the chart are genuinely the same drawing — it is an allocation ring because a misal seen from above is one. Steel is also the most Indian detail available: everything is served in it.

Risk: a donut chart is the single most used shape in fintech. The steel rim and the palette are what keep it from being every other portfolio app’s icon — decide whether that’s enough.
